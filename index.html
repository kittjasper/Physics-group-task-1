Experiment 1 Uncertainty of measurements

Uncertainty
of measurements.

1. Introduction and purpose

The experiment aimed to determine the uncertainty of different measurements by using appropriate measuring instruments and considering their least count. By measuring different objects and quantities, we were able to observe how the precision of an instrument affects the reliability of the measurements.

Experiment 2 / 01 Introduction

From object
to evidence.

The experiment was conducted to determine the measurements, mass, volume, and density of a metal ball and a metal cylinder. A ruler and vernier caliper were used to measure the length and diameter of the objects, while a triple beam balance was used to measure their mass. The collected measurements were then used to calculate the volume and density of each object.

The purpose of the experiment was to develop accuracy and proper techniques in measuring physical properties and to understand the relationship between mass, volume, and density.

01

Least count

The smallest value an instrument can reliably resolve. For this record: ruler = 0.1 cm, vernier caliper = 0.01 cm, balance = 0.1 g.

02

Sample cylinder math

With d = 1.50 cm, h = 4.00 cm, and m = 55.8 g: V = π(0.75)²(4.00) = 7.07 cm³; ρ = 55.8 ÷ 7.07 = 7.89 g/cm³.

03

Accuracy & error

Percent error compares an experimental value with an accepted value: |experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100. For 7.89 g/cm³ vs 7.87 g/cm³: error = 0.25%.

04 Reflection

What the
process taught us.

Challenge

Aligning the caliper with the widest part of the ball and keeping the cylinder perfectly vertical required patience.

Error encountered

Parallax, zero error, and small balance fluctuations could shift a reading. These were checked before recording values.

How we ensured precision

We took repeated measurements, read the scale at eye level, zeroed instruments, and recorded only the appropriate significant figures.

05 Conclusion

Final observation

Good science
begins with a
good reading.

Accuracy and precision are essential in scientific measurements because they ensure that the data is correct and reliable. Accuracy refers to how close a measured value is to the accepted value, while precision is the consistency or reproducibility of repeated trials under the same conditions. In the real world, these parameters help prevent random errors and ensure that other experimenters or scientists can reach the correct conclusions from our experiment. In conclusion, comprehensive measurement techniques can turn a raw observation into accurate, precise, and reliable data that can be effectively used in different real-world fields.
